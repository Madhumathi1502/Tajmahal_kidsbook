## EDUCATIONAL CONTENT

### HOW DOES A DOME STAY UP?

The Taj Mahal's most famous feature is its large central dome.

A dome is a curved structure that covers a large space without needing a column in the middle to hold it up.

For a dome to stay up, the building beneath it must:

✅ Have a **wide, stable base** to spread the weight

✅ Use **arches** that direct weight downward and outward

✅ Be **balanced** — weight distributed evenly on all sides

❌ A very **narrow base** cannot support a heavy dome

❌ A **lopsided** structure will lean or fail

### WHAT IS THE TAJ MAHAL MADE FROM?

The Taj Mahal was built using different materials for different purposes:

**White Marble**
The exterior of the mausoleum and the raised platform are covered in white marble. This marble came from **Makrana** in the state of Rajasthan, India. It is prized for its quality and bright appearance.

**Red Sandstone**
The mosque, the jawab (mirror building), the great gateway, and much of the outer complex are built from **red sandstone**. This gives these buildings their warm, reddish colour.

**Semiprecious Stones**
The decorative inlay work on the marble uses coloured semiprecious stones — including lapis lazuli (blue), carnelian (orange-red), jasper, malachite (green), and others. These stones were brought from different regions.

---

## EDUCATIONAL CONTENT

### THE ART OF STONE INLAY

The surfaces of the Taj Mahal are not plain white marble. They are covered in intricate patterns — flowers, leaves, geometric shapes — all made from tiny pieces of carefully cut and shaped stones.

Artisans would:

1. Design the pattern
2. Cut tiny grooves into the marble
3. Shape each small stone piece to fit perfectly
4. Set the stones into the grooves — so precisely that the joins are almost invisible

The result is a surface that looks like it was painted — but is actually made from stone.

The stones used include:
- **Lapis lazuli** (deep blue — from distant regions)
- **Carnelian** (red-orange)
- **Jasper** (various earthy colours)
- **Malachite** (deep green)
- **Onyx** (black/white)

## EDUCATIONAL CONTENT

### CALLIGRAPHY ON THE TAJ MAHAL

The Taj Mahal is decorated with calligraphic inscriptions. These are passages from the **Quran** — the Islamic holy scripture.

The calligraphy on the Taj Mahal is not painted. It is created using the same **stone inlay technique** (pietra dura / parchinkari) you learned about on the previous page — dark marble and jasper shapes set into the white marble surface.

The script style used is called **Thuluth** — a classical style of Arabic calligraphy known for its elegant, flowing lines.

Historical evidence identifies **Amanat Khan** as the master calligrapher who designed the inscriptions. He is believed to have signed his name inside the tomb — an unusual honour that shows how highly his skill was valued.

One remarkable feature: the letters near the top of the arches are made **larger** than the letters at the bottom. This was a deliberate technique — when you look up at the inscription from below, the different sizes make the letters appear uniform.

## EDUCATIONAL CONTENT

### GETTING MATERIALS TO AGRA

The Taj Mahal was built using materials from different places.

**White marble** came from **Makrana**, in the state of Rajasthan — hundreds of kilometres from Agra.

**Semiprecious stones** for the inlay work were brought from various regions.

**Red sandstone** was available more locally.

Moving heavy stone and materials across long distances in the 17th century required:

📦 **Planning** — organising what was needed and when

🐘 **Elephants** — used to transport heavy loads

⛵ **Boats** — rivers were used as transport routes where possible

### THE TAJ MAHAL'S WATER SYSTEM

The gardens and fountains of the Taj Mahal needed water — and a lot of it.

Water was drawn from the **Yamuna River** using systems that lifted it upward and moved it through the complex.

From the river, water was moved into **large elevated storage tanks**. From these tanks, it flowed through underground **pipes and channels** to reach the garden fountains and the central pool.

The system had to be designed so that all the fountains in the garden received water at the same pressure — otherwise some fountains would be strong and others weak.

This kind of careful engineering was essential to make the garden work as designed.

### INSCRIPTIONS AS HISTORICAL EVIDENCE

The Taj Mahal's surfaces carry calligraphic inscriptions — passages from the Quran, written in **Thuluth script**, created using the stone inlay technique.

When historians study inscriptions, they don't only read the words. They also observe:

- **Placement** — Where on the building are the inscriptions? What does their position suggest?
- **Scale** — How large are the letters? Do they change size in different locations?
- **Consistency** — Does the style stay the same throughout, or does it change?
- **Borders** — Are the inscriptions framed or decorated?

**One remarkable engineering detail:** Historical and scholarly sources note that the calligraphy on the Taj Mahal was designed so that the letters appear **uniform in size** when viewed from below — even though they actually increase in size as they go higher up the wall. This optical correction was a deliberate design choice.
